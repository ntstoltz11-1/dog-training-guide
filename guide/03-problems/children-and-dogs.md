# Children and dogs

**Evidence for this page:** Consensus. The safety rules are standard across veterinary behavior and child-safety organizations; the bite-risk patterns are drawn from clinical experience and bite-report data.
**See a vet first?** Yes if the dog has shown any discomfort around children. Pain lowers tolerance for the unpredictable handling children do.

## Why this page exists

Most dog bites to children come from a dog the child knows, at home, during ordinary moments: hugging, lying on the dog, approaching while it eats or sleeps, taking a toy. The dog nearly always warned first and nobody saw it. This page is about seeing it.

## The rules every household needs

1. **Supervise actively or separate.** A parent in the same room looking at a phone is not supervision. If you cannot watch, the dog goes behind a gate or in its space.
2. **The dog's space is sacred.** Crate, bed, or a corner: the dog goes there to be left alone, and children never follow.
3. **No hugging, no kissing the face, no climbing on or lying on the dog.** Dogs tolerate this; almost none enjoy it. Tolerance runs out.
4. **Nobody wakes a sleeping dog.** Call its name from a distance instead.
5. **Food, chews, and toys are off limits.** Children do not approach a dog that has something, and do not feed from the table.
6. **Teach "be a tree."** When a dog is too excited, the child stands still, arms folded, looking at their feet. Movement and squealing make it worse.
7. **Teach the child to invite, not grab.** Pat your leg, let the dog come to you, pet the chest or side, stop after three seconds and see if the dog asks for more.

## What to watch for

Read `guide/01-start-here/body-language-and-threshold.md` and look for these around children in particular: the dog turning its head away, lip licking, yawning, getting up and leaving, a closed mouth, "whale eye", freezing. Each is the dog saying "enough". Act on the first one, before the growl.

If the dog growls at a child: thank the dog silently, remove the child calmly, and change the setup so it does not happen again. Never punish the growl. See `aggression.md`.

## Do this (for the dog)

1. Pair children with good things from a distance: a child appears, the dog gets chicken. Card: `guide/04-protocols/counterconditioning-and-desensitization.md`.
2. Teach a solid "go to your mat" so the dog has somewhere to be during chaos. Card: `guide/04-protocols/relaxation-protocol.md`.
3. Build handling tolerance with cooperative care, by adults first. `cooperative-care.md`.
4. Give the dog daily time away from the children: a walk, a chew behind a gate, a nap in its space.

## Do this (for the child)

- Make the child part of training in safe ways: tossing treats on the floor, asking for a sit from a distance, hiding treats for the dog to find.
- Praise the child for giving the dog space. It is the skill that prevents bites.

## Avoid this

- **"They are best friends, the dog lets her do anything"**: the dog that lets a child do anything is the dog that bites one day with no warning anyone noticed.
- **Letting the dog "learn to tolerate" rough handling**: tolerance is not consent, and it wears thin.
- **Leaving babies and toddlers on the floor with a dog**, even a gentle one, even briefly.

## Get help if

- Any growl, snap, or bite toward a child
- The dog avoids the children or leaves rooms when they enter (the dog is telling you it is struggling)
- A new baby is coming and the dog has any history of guarding or fear
