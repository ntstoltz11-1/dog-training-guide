# Multi-dog homes and introducing dogs

**Evidence for this page:** Consensus. Practitioner protocols; no controlled trials on introduction methods.
**See a vet first?** If a dog that used to get along with housemates has started fighting. Pain and sensory loss are common causes of new conflict between familiar dogs.

## What it is

Two kinds of problem live here. **Introductions**: bringing a new dog into a home with a resident dog, or arranging a first meeting. **Conflict between housemates**: dogs that have lived together and now stiffen, growl, or fight.

## Introducing a new dog

**Before the dogs meet**
- Remove toys, chews, and food bowls from shared spaces. Most first fights are over an item.
- Set up gates or pens so the dogs can be separated at any time, and will be, for the first days.
- Plan the first meeting on neutral ground, not at the house.

**Do this**
1. **Parallel walk.** Two handlers, two dogs, same direction, far enough apart that both are loose and sniffing the ground. Walk for 10 to 15 minutes. Gradually let the distance close as long as both stay relaxed.
2. **Brief sniffs, then move on.** When both are relaxed close together, allow a few seconds of sniffing, then call them apart and keep walking. Short and sweet; do not let a greeting drag on into stiffness.
3. **Into the home, newcomer first in the yard, then resident joins** on leash. Then indoors with leashes dragging, no items on the floor.
4. **Separate when unsupervised** for at least the first one to two weeks. Feed apart. Sleep apart.
5. **Watch both dogs' body language**, not just the new one. A resident dog that goes quiet and leaves rooms is struggling too.
6. **Build freedom slowly.** Supervised time together grows; unsupervised time comes last, after weeks of calm.

Starting numbers are starting points. Some pairs take an afternoon, some take months.

## Conflict between housemates

**Assess**
- What triggers it: food, toys, doorways, a person, excitement at the door, one dog being touched?
- Who starts it and how (stare, stiffen, body block)?
- Any injuries? Bite level on the scale in `aggression.md`.
- Vet check for both, especially the older dog.

**Do this**
1. **Manage the trigger out.** Feed apart, remove contested items, gate doorways, greet visitors one dog at a time.
2. **Reward calm proximity.** Both dogs on mats a few metres apart, treats for settling. Close the distance over weeks.
3. **Teach a strong recall and "go to mat"** for each dog separately, so you can split them with words before a fight.
4. **Lower overall arousal**: more sleep, fewer high-energy group games, structured play with rules.
5. **Keep a log** of incidents: date, trigger, who, how bad. Patterns appear fast.

## If a fight happens

- Do not reach between the dogs. Most owner bites happen here.
- Make a loud noise, throw a blanket, or push a barrier (a chair, a board) between them.
- Separate completely, check for injuries, and keep them apart for at least a day.
- A second fight with injuries is a professional's job.

## Avoid this

- **"Let them sort out who is boss"**: fights that work get repeated. Dominance is not the useful frame; see `guide/02-methods/dominance-and-compulsion.md`.
- **Punishing the dog that growled**: you remove the warning from a dog that lives with the other one.
- **Favouring one dog to "keep the peace"**: it often raises tension. Treat both consistently.
- **Getting a second dog to fix the first dog's anxiety**: sometimes it helps a lonely dog; it does not fix fear or separation panic, and it can double the problem.

## How long it takes

Introductions: days to weeks for most pairs. Resolving conflict between housemates: months, and some pairs are safely managed rather than cured.

## Get help if

- Any fight that breaks skin
- Fights are increasing in frequency or intensity
- One dog is losing weight, hiding, or avoiding rooms
