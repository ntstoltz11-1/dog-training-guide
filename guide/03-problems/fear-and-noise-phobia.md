# Fear, anxiety, and noise phobia

**Evidence for this page:** Consensus for the behavior plan; Strong for medication in noise fear (randomized trials exist).
**See a vet first?** Yes. Pain lowers a dog's tolerance for everything, and medication decisions need a vet.

## What it is

Fear is a response to something present: a stranger, a vacuum, a thunderclap. Anxiety is the dread of something that might happen. Noise phobia (fireworks, storms, gunshots) is the most common severe fear. Signs: trembling, hiding, panting, pacing, drooling, refusing food, trying to escape, destructiveness around doors and windows.

## Before you start

- **Safe haven.** A room or crate the dog chooses, with bedding, water, and a chew. Never lock a panicking dog in; the point is a place to go, not a cage.
- **Sound masking.** A fan, white noise, or music during storms and fireworks.
- Keep a simple log: what happened, how bad (1 to 5), how long to recover. [Measurement sheet: how to tell if training is working](../06-tools/measurement-sheet.md).

## Do this

1. For sudden fears (a person, an object), use counterconditioning: the scary thing appears at a distance where the dog is still relaxed, great food follows, repeat. Card: [Counterconditioning and desensitization (CC/DS)](../04-protocols/counterconditioning-and-desensitization.md).
2. For noise fear, use recordings at a volume so low the dog barely notices, paired with food or play. Raise the volume over many sessions. This works better for fireworks and gunshots than for thunderstorms, which come with pressure changes and static the recording cannot copy.
3. Build a daily calm routine: the Relaxation Protocol teaches settling on a mat while mild distractions happen. Card: [Relaxation Protocol](../04-protocols/relaxation-protocol.md).
4. During a real event, comfort your dog. You cannot reinforce fear by being kind; fear is not a behavior the dog chooses. Stay calm, stay close if the dog wants that, and let it hide if it prefers.
5. Ask your vet about medication **before** the next fireworks season, not during it.

## Medication, in plain terms

| Situation | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|
| Fireworks and sudden noise | A gel applied to the gums (dexmedetomidine, sold as Sileo) was clearly better than placebo in a randomized trial of 182 dogs: 72% of owners rated the effect good or excellent vs 37% on placebo. A tablet (imepitoin, sold as Pexion) also beat placebo in a trial of 238 dogs and is approved for this use in both the US and the EU; about half the dogs on it were wobbly or hungrier, and a few became less inhibited, so ask your vet about side effects. **Strong.** |
| Thunderstorms | A small crossover trial of gabapentin (18 dogs) reduced fear on average, but 3 dogs got worse. **Thin.** |
| Daily anxiety | Daily medication (fluoxetine or clomipramine) takes 4 to 6 weeks to work and is combined with training. **Moderate.** |

No doses here. Your vet chooses the drug and the amount. See [Talking to your vet about behavior medication](../06-tools/medication-conversation-guide.md).

## Avoid this

- **Forcing the dog to "face its fear"** (flooding): it can make the fear permanent.
- **Punishing fearful behavior** (hiding, whining, growling): the dog learns you are also dangerous.
- **Waiting for the dog to "grow out of it"**: noise fears usually get worse with each exposure.

## How long it takes

Situational medication works within an hour. Counterconditioning takes weeks to months. Noise fear is managed more than cured for many dogs; the goal is a dog that copes, not a dog that enjoys fireworks.

## Get help if

- The dog injures itself or damages doors or windows trying to escape
- Fear is spreading to new things
- The dog cannot eat or settle most days
