# Leash reactivity (barking and lunging at dogs or people)

**Evidence for this page:** Consensus: the protocols are standard practice among veterinary behaviorists and certified trainers; controlled trials are lacking. The evidence against using corrections for it is Moderate.
**See a vet first?** Yes. Pain and poor eyesight both drive reactivity. See [`first-rule-see-the-vet.md`](first-rule-see-the-vet.md).

## What it is

Your dog sees another dog or a person on a walk and explodes: barking, lunging, growling, spinning. Two very different feelings cause the same picture. **Fear**: the dog wants the thing to go away. **Frustration**: the dog wants to get to the thing and the leash will not let it. The plan is similar for both, but the "frustrated greeter" needs more work on calm greetings and the fearful dog needs more distance.

## Before you start

- Find your dog's **threshold distance**: how far away a trigger can be while the dog can still eat and look at you. Write it down. That is your starting line.
- For two weeks, avoid triggers inside that distance. Walk at quiet times, cross the street, use parked cars and hedges as screens. This is not avoidance forever; it stops the dog rehearsing.
- Teach an emergency U-turn at home: say "this way!", turn, and feed as the dog follows. Practice until it is reflex.
- Fit a front-clip harness. Rule out any collar that tightens.

## Do this

1. Start at or beyond threshold distance with a trigger in view.
2. The moment your dog **looks** at the trigger, say your marker ("yes") and feed. Repeat every time the dog looks. You are teaching "a dog appears, chicken happens." Card: [`guide/04-protocols/look-at-that.md`](../04-protocols/look-at-that.md).
3. When your dog starts glancing at the trigger and then turning to you on its own, you have progress. Feed that too.
4. Over sessions, close the distance by a few steps at a time. If the dog stops eating or stiffens, you went too fast; back up.
5. Add pattern games for moments you cannot control, like a dog appearing round a corner. Card: [`guide/04-protocols/pattern-games.md`](../04-protocols/pattern-games.md).
6. If you can arrange a helper with a calm dog, run set-ups where your dog explores on a long line and chooses to move away. Card: [`guide/04-protocols/bat-setups.md`](../04-protocols/bat-setups.md).

Starting numbers: sessions of 5 to 10 minutes, 2 to 4 times a week, with easy walks in between. Adjust to your dog.

## Avoid this

- **Leash corrections, prong, or shock**: the dog learns that other dogs predict pain, which deepens the fear. In clinic data, confrontational methods were linked to aggressive responses.
- **Making the dog sit and face the trigger**: staring and stillness are often the moment before a lunge. Movement helps.
- **Waiting for the dog to look away before rewarding**: the dog learns to stare harder first. Reward the glance.
- **"Socializing" by forcing greetings**: a reactive dog does not need more dogs in its face.

## How long it takes

Management gains in days. Real change in threshold distance over 3 to 12 months. A good sign: the dog recovers faster after a surprise. A plateau usually means you are working too close, or the dog is in pain or over-tired from other stress.

## Get help if

- Your dog has bitten or tried to bite a person or dog
- You cannot find any distance at which the dog can eat
- No change in threshold distance after 8 to 12 weeks
