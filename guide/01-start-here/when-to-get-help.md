# When to get help, and who to call

Dog training is unregulated in most countries. Anyone can call themselves a trainer or behaviorist. This page tells you when to stop and call someone, and how to pick the right someone.

**Evidence:** Consensus. The CCPDT requirements below were checked against its own handbook on 2026-10-08; the other bodies' requirements have not yet been checked and can change.

## Call a professional now if

- Your dog has bitten a person or another animal and broken skin
- There are children in the home and the dog growls, snaps, or guards
- Your dog hurts itself (breaking teeth or nails trying to escape a crate, for example)
- Your dog cannot be left alone without panic (howling, soiling, destruction every time)
- You are afraid of your dog
- You have worked at a problem for 8 to 12 weeks with no measurable progress

## Three kinds of professional

| Who | What they do | When you need them |
|---|---|---|
| **Veterinarian** | Rules out pain and illness; can prescribe medication | First stop for any behavior problem. Around a third of dogs referred for behavior have pain involved. |
| **Veterinary behaviorist** (DACVB in the US; equivalent specialist titles elsewhere) | A vet with years of extra behavior training. Diagnoses, prescribes, and designs the plan | Aggression, severe anxiety, separation panic, anything with bites |
| **Certified trainer or behavior consultant** | Teaches skills and manners; runs the day-to-day protocol with you. Consultants handle behavior problems in cooperation with your vet | Manners, basic skills, reactivity, fear, guarding, with a vet involved for the serious cases |

## Credentials that mean something

| Letters | Issued by | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| CPDT-KA / CPDT-KSA | Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers | KA: 300 logged hours in 3 years (225 hands-on), an attestation from a professional, and a knowledge exam; renews every 3 years. KSA adds a video-assessed hands-on skills test. Only a small minority of KA holders go on to KSA, so ask which one. Bound by a least-intrusive (LIMA) standard. |
| CBCC-KA | Same body | Behavior consulting knowledge exam plus 300 hours of consulting experience; renews every 5 years |
| KPA-CTP | Karen Pryor Academy | Completed a structured reward-based course with graded hands-on work |
| CDBC | International Association of Animal Behavior Consultants | Case studies, experience, exam; bound by a least-intrusive ethics code |
| CAAB / ACAAB | Animal Behavior Society | PhD (CAAB) or master's (ACAAB) in behavior plus supervised experience |
| DACVB | American College of Veterinary Behaviorists | Veterinarian with residency, research, and board exam |

No letters does not mean no skill. But letters mean someone checked.

## Questions to ask before hiring

1. What happens when my dog gets it right? (Expect: reward.)
2. What happens when my dog gets it wrong? (Expect: easier setup, management, redirect. Walk away from: correction, pop, "pressure", stim.)
3. Do you use prong, choke, or shock collars? (Expect: no.)
4. Will you work with my vet? (Expect: yes.)
5. Can I watch a class before signing up? (Expect: yes.)
6. What is your plan if this does not work? (Expect: a referral path, not a guarantee.)

Red flags: guarantees, "pack leader" or "dominance" language, refusing to explain methods, board-and-train with no owner involvement, any tool worn by the dog that you are not allowed to see used.

## What a good first appointment looks like

A history of the problem, a look at video you bring, a check of the dog's body language, a management plan for the next two weeks, and a way to measure progress. Advice on the first visit should be mostly about preventing the behavior, not fixing it yet.
