# The basics in 10 minutes

How dogs learn, in plain words. Everything else in this guide rests on these ideas.

## Two kinds of learning

**Learning by consequence.** Dogs repeat what pays off and drop what does not. If sitting gets a treat, sitting happens more. If jumping makes you turn away, jumping fades. This is the engine of all training.

**Learning by association.** Dogs also learn that one thing predicts another. The leash predicts a walk, so the leash gets exciting. A stranger predicts something scary, so strangers get scary. You cannot "command" an emotion, but you can change what a thing predicts. That is the engine of fixing fear and reactivity.

## Four ways consequences work

| What you do | What happens to the behavior | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Add something the dog wants | More of it | Treat for sitting |
| Take away something the dog wants | Less of it | Play stops when teeth touch skin |
| Add something unpleasant | Less of it | Leash jerk, shock, yelling |
| Take away something unpleasant | More of it | Pressure stops when the dog complies |

This guide builds on the first two rows. The last two work in the short term but carry risks: fear, aggression, and a dog that shuts down. The methods section covers the evidence.

## Timing is everything

The reward has to arrive within about half a second of the behavior, or the dog connects it to whatever it did next. That is why trainers use a **marker**: a word like "yes" or a click that means "that, right there, treat coming." The marker buys you time to reach for the food. A click and a word work equally well; pick one and be consistent. See [`guide/04-protocols/marker-training-basics.md`](../04-protocols/marker-training-basics.md).

## Rewards have value, and value depends on difficulty

Kibble works in the kitchen. It does not work next to a squirrel. Rank your dog's rewards (food, toys, sniffing, chasing, greeting) and match the reward to how hard the moment is. Real-life rewards count: "sit, then I open the door" uses the door as the paycheck.

## Build in small steps

Raise one thing at a time. If you ask the dog to sit for longer, do it closer and with fewer distractions. Trainers call these the three Ds: **distance**, **duration**, **distraction**. Change one, lower the other two.

A useful rule of thumb: if the dog gets it right about 8 times out of 10, make it a little harder. If less than 5 out of 10, make it easier.

## Dogs do not generalize well

A sit in the kitchen is not yet a sit at the park. The dog has to learn it in several places before it "knows" it. Plan for this rather than being surprised by it.

## Short and spaced beats long and daily

In the one area where dogs have been tested directly, short sessions with rest between them produced faster learning than long daily drilling, and the dogs remembered just as well weeks later. Those were laboratory dogs on one task, so take it as a direction, not a rule: a few minutes, several times, with sleep in between.

## Stress shuts learning down

A dog that is too scared or too excited to eat is not learning what you intend. The dog can be "over threshold" (past the point where it can think). Lowering the pressure is not giving up; it is the training. Stress also stacks: a vet visit, then visitors, then a dog on the walk can add up so the dog explodes at the third one. See [`body-language-and-threshold.md`](body-language-and-threshold.md).

## Extinction and the burst

If a behavior stops paying, it gets worse before it gets better. A dog that always got attention for barking will bark louder when attention stops. That spike is normal and brief if you hold steady and reward something else instead.

## Dominance is not the explanation

The idea that dogs misbehave to be "alpha" came from studies of unrelated wolves kept in captivity. Wild wolf packs are families. Decades of behavior science explain dog behavior through learning, fear, and what things predict, without any need for a status battle. Methods built on "being the pack leader" are covered, and not recommended, in [`guide/02-methods/dominance-and-compulsion.md`](../02-methods/dominance-and-compulsion.md).
