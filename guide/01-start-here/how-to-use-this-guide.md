# How to use this guide

Five minutes. Read this once and the rest of the guide will make sense.

## Who this is for

Anyone living with a dog. You do not need experience, special equipment, or a particular kind of dog. The guide is not written for one breed or age; it covers puppies, adolescents, adopted adults, and seniors.

## What this guide believes, and why

Three ideas run through every page:

1. **Reward what you want.** Dogs repeat what pays off. Every method here builds on that, because the research consistently finds reward-based training works at least as well as methods that rely on pain or fear, with fewer side effects.
2. **Fear and pain stop learning.** A scared dog or a sore dog cannot learn what you are trying to teach. That is why the first step for any behavior problem is a vet visit, and why we keep the dog calm enough to think.
3. **Measure, do not guess.** A short note after each session beats a vague feeling that things are "getting better". The tools section gives you simple ways to do this.

## How to read a page

Every problem page has the same sections in the same order: **What it is**, **Before you start**, **Do this**, **Avoid this**, **How long it takes**, **Get help if**. Skim the headers, jump to what you need.

## The evidence labels

Dog training advice is often confident and unsupported. So every page says how strong the support is:

- **Strong**: several good studies agree.
- **Moderate**: one controlled study, or consistent surveys.
- **Consensus**: experts agree, but nobody has run a formal trial.
- **Thin**: a case report or less. Treat as a starting point.

Most named training techniques are "Consensus". That does not mean they fail. It means the formal trials have not been done. We say so rather than pretend.

The last line of every page looks like `Evidence: C-01, C-03`. Those are row numbers in `evidence/claims.md`, where you can see the exact study or statement. Ignore it unless you want to check our work.

## Numbers are starting points

When a page says "start 20 steps away" or "hold for 5 seconds", that is a sensible place to begin. Your dog sets the real number. If your dog cannot eat, cannot respond to a cue it knows, or is staring hard at something, you are too close or going too fast.

## What this guide is not

It is not veterinary advice, legal advice, or a substitute for a qualified professional when a dog is dangerous or in distress. `when-to-get-help.md` tells you who to call and how to choose.
