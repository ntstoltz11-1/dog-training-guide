# Reading your dog: body language and threshold

The single most useful skill in this guide. Every behavior protocol depends on knowing when your dog is calm enough to learn.

**Evidence:** Consensus. The signals below are agreed on by veterinary behaviorists and trainers. Formal studies on what each signal "means" are limited, so read them as stress indicators, not as a dictionary.

## What "threshold" means

Threshold is the point past which your dog can no longer think, eat, or respond to you. Below it, the dog notices the trigger and stays loose. Above it, the dog is barking, lunging, frozen, or trying to flee. All of the training in this guide happens below threshold. If you are over it, you are not training; you are just getting through the moment.

## Signs your dog is relaxed

- Loose, wiggly body; weight evenly on all four feet
- Soft eyes, mouth slightly open, ears in their natural position
- Takes treats gently and chews them
- Can look at a trigger and then look away or back at you

## Early stress signals (the quiet ones)

These come first and are easy to miss:

- Lip licking with no food around
- Yawning when not tired
- Turning the head or body away
- Sniffing the ground suddenly
- Scratching, shaking off as if wet
- "Whale eye" (white of the eye showing)
- Closed mouth after it was open
- Taking treats hard, or refusing them

One of these alone can mean nothing. Several together, or a change from a moment ago, means your dog is working hard to cope. Add distance or end the session.

## Escalation (the loud ones)

Stiffness, staring, growling, raised hackles, lunging, snapping, biting. These are the top of the ladder. Most dogs climb it in order and give the quiet signals first. **Punishing a growl removes the warning without removing the feeling**, and the dog may skip straight to a bite next time.

## Overarousal is not the same as fear

An excited dog that cannot settle (jumping, mouthing, spinning, zooming, grabbing the leash) is also over threshold, just in the other direction. The fix is the same: less intensity, more structure, and a rest.

## Trigger stacking

Stress adds up over hours or a day. A dog that handled the vet, then visitors, then a thunderstorm may lose it at a bicycle it normally ignores. After a hard day, plan a quiet one. Trainers call this trigger stacking; it is widely observed, not formally measured.

## How to use this

1. Before any training session, note where your dog is: relaxed, quiet signals, or escalating.
2. Train only when relaxed or at the first quiet signal.
3. When two or more quiet signals appear, add distance or stop.
4. Write down the distance or situation where your dog tips over. That number is your starting point for every protocol, and watching it shrink is how you know training is working.
