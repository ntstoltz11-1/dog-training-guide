# Reward-based training

**Verdict:** Recommended as the default.
**Evidence:** Moderate to Strong: the largest and most consistent body of evidence in dog training, though mostly from surveys and a small number of controlled field studies.

## What it is

You reward the behavior you want, so it happens more. You prevent or ignore the behavior you do not want, so it fades. Mistakes are information about the setup, not something to correct. The dog is never hurt, startled, or pressured.

## How it works

Dogs repeat what pays. A **marker** (a click or "yes") pinpoints the exact moment, then a treat, toy, or real-life reward follows. Four basic ways to get a behavior to happen so you can reward it:

| Technique | What you do | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Luring** | Guide the dog with food in your hand | Fade the food fast, within a few repetitions, or the dog waits to see it |
| **Capturing** | Wait for the behavior to happen naturally, mark it | Slow for rare behaviors; great for calm and settling |
| **Shaping** | Reward small steps toward the goal | Keep steps tiny; if the dog stalls, you lumped too much |
| **Targeting** | Teach a nose or paw touch, then move the target | A versatile building block for recall, positions, and cooperative care |

Step-by-step: [Marker training basics](../04-protocols/marker-training-basics.md).

## What the research says

- Dogs trained mostly with rewards are rated more obedient by their owners and show fewer problem behaviors than dogs trained mostly with punishment (surveys of several hundred owners).
- In the direct field comparisons, reward-trained dogs responded faster and needed fewer repeated cues than shock-collar-trained dogs, and showed fewer stress signs.
- Dogs at reward-based schools show fewer stress behaviors and look at their owners more than dogs at correction-based schools.
- Most of this is correlational. Owners choose their methods, so dogs with problems may attract punishment rather than the other way around. The direction is consistent across study types, which is why it counts.

## Strengths

- Low risk of fear, aggression, or shutdown
- Builds a dog that offers behavior and enjoys working with you
- Works for every age and every problem in this guide, including fear and aggression
- Easy for everyone in the household to do the same way

## Risks and side effects

- Frustration if rewards come too slowly in a hard environment
- Extra calories if treats are not budgeted (use part of the daily food; about a tenth of calories as treats)
- A dog that only performs when it sees food, if luring is faded too late

## Skill needed

Moderate. The skills are timing, how often you reward, and raising difficulty in small steps. All are learnable in weeks. The classic beginner error is bribery: showing the treat before the cue instead of after the behavior.

## Legal notes

None.

## Best fit

Everything. Puppies, adults, seniors, fearful dogs, aggressive dogs (with a professional), cooperative care, manners.

## Common mistakes

- **Bribing**: food visible before the behavior. The food comes after the marker.
- **Fading too late**: three or four lured repetitions, then the hand signal alone.
- **Too few rewards**: in a hard place, reward every second or two until the dog can think.
- **No plan for emergencies**: rewards do not stop a dog mid-charge. Teach an interrupter ("this way!") and use management.
