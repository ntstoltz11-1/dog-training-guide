# Balanced training

**Verdict:** Described for comparison. Not recommended.
**Evidence:** Moderate: one controlled field study (reported in two papers) and several welfare studies, all pointing the same way, with one published statistical critique that the authors dispute.

## What it is

"Balanced" trainers use rewards to teach a behavior, then corrections to enforce it: leash pops, prong (pinch) collars, or shock collars (sold as "e-collars" or "remote collars", often set to a low "stim" the dog works to switch off). The claim is that rewards alone cannot make a behavior reliable around real distractions.

## How it works

Two mechanisms. The correction makes the unwanted behavior less likely by adding something unpleasant. The low-level stim works by pressure that stops when the dog complies. Both can change behavior quickly. The question is what else they change.

## What the research says

- In a government-funded field study of 63 dogs with recall and chasing problems, dogs trained with shock collars by industry-approved trainers did not perform better than dogs trained with rewards. Owners in all groups reported improvement. Shock-collar dogs were tense more often, yawned more, and explored less. Stress hormone levels did not differ between groups.
- A second analysis of the same dogs, looking at the training videos, found reward-trained dogs obeyed the first "come" or "sit" more often (about 83% of the time versus 71 to 77%) and responded faster. The authors concluded the collars were unnecessary. A later commentary criticized the design and statistics and argued the result could even go the other way; the authors replied defending their analysis. Nobody has re-run the numbers, so treat this as contested, not settled.
- A welfare study comparing reward-only, mixed, and aversive-heavy training schools found that dogs from aversive-heavy schools showed more stress behaviors, bigger stress hormone rises, and a more pessimistic outlook in a mood test than reward-only dogs. Dogs from mixed schools showed more stress behaviors during training, but not the hormone or mood differences. The authors argue that the share of aversives, more than the specific tool, drives the stress.
- No published study has shown a balanced approach beating well-run reward-based training for pet-dog obedience or recall.

## Strengths

- Can suppress a behavior quickly in the hands of a skilled trainer
- Some working-dog programs report value for specific avoidance tasks (not studied in pet dogs)

## Risks and side effects

- Fear of the handler, the place, or whatever the dog was looking at when corrected
- Suppressed warning signals: a dog punished for growling may bite without warning
- Stress behaviors and raised stress hormones during and after training
- Escalation: the setting creeps up as the dog habituates
- Used on a fearful dog, corrections treat the symptom and deepen the fear

## Skill needed

Very high for safe use. Mistimed rewards cost a treat. Mistimed corrections teach the wrong lesson and cannot be taken back.

## Legal notes (as of 2026-10-08)

- **Wales:** shock collar use banned since 2010.
- **England:** not banned. Regulations drafted to start 2024-02-01 were never brought into force. A government consultation was promised in December 2025.
- **Scotland:** not banned; a proposed ban was voted down 84 to 28 on 2025-01-23.
- **Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Denmark, Sweden, and Norway** ban shock-collar use by statute (Norway and Switzerland allow narrow, licensed exceptions). Finland reports a ban. Check your local law; details in [Legal status of training tools](../../evidence/legal-status.md).

## Best fit

This guide does not identify a pet-dog situation where it is the best fit.

## Common mistakes

- Using corrections on fear-based behavior
- "Low level" stim that is quietly raised over weeks
- Treating a quiet, still dog as "calm" when it has shut down
