# Dominance theory and compulsion training

**Verdict:** Not recommended. Described so you can recognize it.
**Evidence:** Strong against the theory; Moderate against the methods (one clinic survey of outcomes).

## What it is

Compulsion (or "traditional") training relies on force: choke-chain jerks, physically placing the dog, "corrections" for mistakes. It is usually justified by **dominance theory**: the idea that your dog misbehaves because it is trying to be the "alpha", and that you must assert pack leadership. Techniques include alpha rolls (pinning the dog on its back), stare-downs, scruff shakes, eating before the dog, and never letting the dog through a door first.

## Where the theory came from, and why it fell apart

- The "alpha wolf" idea came from studies of unrelated wolves forced together in captivity in the 1940s. They fought because they were strangers in a cage.
- The scientist whose 1970 book made the idea famous later studied wild wolves for over a decade and found that packs are families: parents and their offspring, with no dominance battles. He has urged people to drop the term and asked his publisher to stop printing the book.
- Dog behavior scientists have since shown that most "dominant" behavior in pet dogs is better explained by learning, fear, or how much the dog values a resource.
- Veterinary behavior organizations advise against dominance-based methods.

The word "dominance" still has a legitimate narrow meaning in animal behavior: which of two animals usually gets a specific resource. What has been rejected is the idea that your dog is plotting to run your household, and the confrontational methods built on it.

## What the research says about the methods

A survey of owners at a veterinary behavior clinic recorded how dogs responded to each technique:

| Technique | Dogs that responded aggressively |
|---|---|
| Hit or kick | 43% |
| Growl at the dog | 41% |
| Force an item from the mouth | 39% |
| Alpha roll | 31% |
| Stare down | 30% |
| "Dominance down" | 29% |
| Grab jowls and shake | 26% |
| Reward-based methods (food, clicker, "look at me") | Very few |

These were 140 dogs already referred for problems, mostly aggression, and the owners were recalling what happened, so the numbers may be high for the average pet and cannot prove the technique caused the bite. The pattern is the point: confrontation goes with aggression, rewards do not.

## Risks and side effects

- Aggression toward the handler
- Learned helplessness: a dog that stops offering any behavior and looks "calm" but has given up
- Fear of hands, approach, and eye contact that then shows up at the vet and with children

## Skill needed

Easy to apply, which is part of the problem. The risk does not fall with experience.

## Legal notes

Some techniques (hitting, kicking, hanging by the collar) may breach animal welfare law in many jurisdictions.

## Best fit

None for pet dogs, by current veterinary and scientific consensus.

## How to recognize it

Words to listen for: alpha, pack leader, dominance, "he knows what he did", "he is being stubborn", "show him who is boss". Then read [When to get help, and who to call](../01-start-here/when-to-get-help.md) and keep looking.
