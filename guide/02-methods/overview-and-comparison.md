# Training methods: overview and comparison

One page to compare the main styles before you pick a trainer, a class, or an approach.

**Bottom line:** reward-based training, run within a "least intrusive first" decision framework, is the default this guide recommends. In every controlled comparison published so far, it works at least as well as methods using pain or fear, with fewer side effects. The evidence is consistent but mostly from surveys and a few field studies; there are very few randomized trials. That is a strong signal, not final proof, and we say so.

## The six styles at a glance

| Style | How it works | Does it work? | Risks | Legal issues | Our verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Reward-based** | Reward what you want; remove rewards or manage what you do not | Equal or faster than aversive methods in the controlled comparisons | Low. Possible frustration or extra calories | None | **Default** |
| **LIMA / Humane Hierarchy** | A decision ladder: health and environment first, then rewards, aversives only as a last resort | Inherits reward-based evidence | Low. Critics note "last resort" can creep | None | **Recommended framework** |
| **Balanced** | Rewards to teach, corrections (leash pop, prong, shock) to enforce | Not better than reward-based in any controlled test | Fear, stress, aggression risk, suppressed warnings | Shock collars banned in Wales and several European countries | Described, not recommended |
| **Compulsion / dominance** | Force and "pack leader" theory: alpha rolls, stare-downs, physical corrections | No evidence of advantage; the scientist who popularized the "alpha wolf" idea has disowned it | High: owners reported aggressive responses to 26 to 43% of uses of these techniques in one clinic survey | Some techniques may breach animal welfare law | Not recommended |
| **Relationship, games, and choice** (Control Unleashed, BAT, Do As I Do) | Structure, predictability, and letting the dog make choices below threshold | Practitioner reports are good; formal trials are few | Low. Needs skill at reading the dog | None | Useful, especially for reactive and anxious dogs |
| **Behavior analysis framing** | Measure, define the function of the behavior, change what maintains it | Strong science base; few dog-specific trials | Low if reward-led; can miss emotion and pain if applied mechanically | None | The measuring backbone for everything else |

## How to read this table

- "Does it work?" compares acquisition speed, reliability, and whether the behavior holds up in new places.
- "Risks" means side effects seen in studies: stress behaviors, higher stress hormones, more pessimistic mood in tests, and aggression toward the handler.
- The welfare finding is consistent across many study types. The efficacy finding ("aversives are never faster") rests on one government-funded field study of 63 dogs, reported in two papers by the same research group, and a published critique argues its statistics could change the result; the authors disagree. Treat the welfare conclusion as well supported and the "never more effective in any situation" conclusion as open.

## Where the real disagreements are

1. **Is the research good enough to conclude anything?** One 2017 review said the pre-2017 literature was too weak for firm conclusions. Another 2017 review of the same literature concluded aversives risk welfare and recommended reward-based methods. Both are right about different things: the methods are weak, and the direction of the findings is consistent.
2. **Are there tasks where shock collars help?** Some working-dog contexts (training dogs to avoid dangerous wildlife) report value. No controlled comparison exists for pet dogs.
3. **Does LIMA permit too much?** Some force-free trainers reject the bottom rungs of the Humane Hierarchy entirely. Others say a framework that names aversives as a last resort is more honest than pretending they never happen.

## How to choose

- You want to teach skills and manners: reward-based.
- Your dog is fearful, reactive, or anxious: reward-based plus the games and choice approaches, with a vet involved.
- A trainer proposes corrections: ask what the dog did to get them, and read [Balanced training](balanced-training.md) first.
- A trainer talks about dominance or being the alpha: read [Dominance theory and compulsion training](dominance-and-compulsion.md), then keep looking.
