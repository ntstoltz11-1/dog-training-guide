# Evidence-Based Dog Training and Behavior Modification: A Comparative Reference Guide

Reward-based training, run under a LIMA / Humane Hierarchy decision framework, is the best-supported default for pet dogs. Across the observational, quasi-experimental and controlled field studies available, it performs at least as well as aversive or "balanced" methods for everyday obedience and recall, and it carries fewer welfare risks. The aversive methods have not been shown to offer any compensating gain in efficacy. That said, the evidence base is mostly correlational, uses small samples, and rests on very few randomized trials. A rigorous reference should therefore present this as a strong, consistent signal rather than settled proof.

## TL;DR

- **Default method:** Use positive reinforcement (marker training, shaping, luring, capturing) inside the LIMA / Humane Hierarchy framework.
  - Studies agree that aversive methods bring more stress behaviors, higher post-training cortisol, more "pessimistic" cognitive bias and more confrontation-triggered aggression, with no reliable gain in obedience or recall (Hiby 2004; Herron 2009; Cooper 2014; China 2020; Vieira de Castro 2020).
- **Behavior problems:** Start with a veterinary exam and a pain workup. Mills et al. (2020, Animals 10:318) give a conservative estimate that "around a third of referred cases involve some form of painful condition," and nearly 80% in some caseloads.
  - Then apply counterconditioning and desensitization with management, plus medication as an adjunct where indicated.
  - Medication evidence is strongest for fluoxetine and clomipramine in separation anxiety and for dexmedetomidine gel in noise aversion. It is thin for situational trazodone and gabapentin.
- **Weak points and corrections:** Several popular claims are weakly supported or contradicted.
  - Dominance/"alpha" theory: Mech 1999 showed wild wolf packs are families.
  - Breed as destiny: in Morrill et al. (2022, Science), using owner surveys for 18,385 dogs, "breed explains just 9% of behavioral variation in individuals."
  - The 3-3-3 decompression rule has no peer-reviewed basis.
  - Contrary to common belief, England's planned 1 February 2024 e-collar ban never came into force. As of October 2026 only Wales has a statutory UK ban.

## Key Findings

1. **Efficacy:** No published study shows aversive tools beating well-applied reward-based training for companion-dog obedience or recall. The best controlled field data (the DEFRA-funded Lincoln project, reported by Cooper 2014 and China 2020) found reward-focused training equal or better on response latency and first-cue compliance.
2. **Welfare:** The aversive-welfare link is consistent across designs: surveys, direct observation, cortisol, cognitive bias. Vieira de Castro 2020 found that the *proportion* of aversives mattered more than the specific tool. That supports the "minimally aversive" logic of LIMA.
3. **Safety:** Confrontational techniques provoke aggression at high rates. In Herron 2009, 26% to 43% of dogs responded aggressively, depending on technique, versus 0% to 6% for non-confrontational methods.
4. **Genuine disagreement exists:** Guilherme Fernandes, Olsson and Vieira de Castro (2017) judged the pre-2017 literature too methodologically weak for firm conclusions. Ziv (2017) concluded aversives jeopardize welfare. China 2020 drew a published methodological critique (Elliffe et al. 2021). Treat the welfare conclusion as well-supported. Treat "e-collars are never more effective in any context" (for example, predator-avoidance training) as unresolved.
5. **Learning schedule:** Short, spaced sessions beat long, daily, massed sessions for acquisition in laboratory dogs (Meyer and Ladewig 2008; Demant et al. 2011). One of the few dosage findings with experimental support.
6. **Development:** Socialization should begin before vaccinations are complete (AVSAB 2008). Adolescence (around 8 months) brings a temporary, caregiver-specific drop in responsiveness (Asher et al. 2020).

## Part 1: Training Philosophies and Methods

### 1.1 Core learning science

**Classical (Pavlovian) conditioning:** the dog learns that one event predicts another. Produces conditioned emotional responses (CERs), the main target in fear and reactivity work.

**Operant conditioning:** behavior changes because of its consequences. The four quadrants:

| Quadrant | Operation | Effect | Dog example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive reinforcement (R+) | Add something wanted | Increases | Treat for sit |
| Negative reinforcement (R-) | Remove something aversive | Increases | E-collar stim stops when dog turns toward handler |
| Positive punishment (P+) | Add something aversive | Decreases | Leash pop, shock, prong correction |
| Negative punishment (P-) | Remove something wanted | Decreases | Play stops when teeth touch skin |

**Extinction:** withholding the maintaining reinforcer. Expect an extinction burst. Pair with differential reinforcement.

**Differential reinforcement:** DRA (alternative behavior), DRI (incompatible behavior), DRO (absence of the behavior for an interval).

**Premack principle:** a more probable behavior (sniffing, chasing) can reinforce a less probable one (eye contact, recall).

**Reinforcement schedules and the matching law:** continuous reinforcement speeds acquisition; variable schedules produce persistence. The matching law (Herrnstein) says animals distribute behavior in proportion to relative reinforcement available. Well established in basic behavior analysis; application to pet dogs inferred.

**Marker timing:** contingency weakens rapidly as delay grows; trainers aim to mark within about half a second. Dog studies comparing clickers with verbal markers are mixed. "Clickers are faster" is thin; "precise timing matters" is strong.

**Criteria setting and splitting:** raise one criterion at a time; the 80% rule of thumb is consensus, not tested; split rather than lump.

**Generalization and proofing (3 Ds):** distance, duration, distraction, raised one at a time.

**Reward value hierarchy:** rank reinforcers per dog; match value to difficulty. Praise alone is generally weaker than food in experimental tests.

**Trigger stacking:** stressors accumulate until a threshold is crossed. Consensus concept, not directly measured in dogs.

**Stress and learning:** moderate arousal supports learning; high arousal and fear impair it. Vieira de Castro 2020 found higher post-training cortisol rises and more stress behaviors in aversive-trained dogs.

**Sleep and consolidation:** human and rodent work plus a few dog EEG studies suggest sleep after training supports consolidation. Dog-specific evidence thin but promising.

**Session spacing:** Meyer and Ladewig (2008), 18 lab dogs: weekly training needed fewer sessions than five-times-weekly. Demant et al. (2011), 44 lab Beagles: 1 to 2 sessions weekly gave steeper acquisition than daily; short sessions beat long; 4-week retention did not differ. Caveats: lab dogs, single tasks. Takeaway: short sessions with rest between, not "train once a week."

### 1.2 Method profiles

#### A. Positive reinforcement / reward-based (R+)

- **Mechanism:** R+ for desired behavior, P- and extinction for unwanted behavior, management to prevent rehearsal. Techniques: luring, capturing, shaping, targeting, marker/clicker training.
- **Evidence:** Hiby, Rooney and Bradshaw (2004, Animal Welfare 13:63-69), 364 owners: obedience correlated with reward-trained tasks, problems with punishment-trained tasks. Blackwell et al. (2008, J Vet Behav 3:207-217), 192 owners: 16% reward only, 72% some P+; reward-only dogs had fewest problems. Deldalle and Gaunet (2014): dogs at an R- school showed more stress behaviors and gazed at owners less.
- **Effectiveness:** equal or faster acquisition in controlled comparisons (China 2020: shorter latencies, more first-cue responses).
- **Welfare risks:** low. Frustration if rate drops; obesity if treats unbudgeted.
- **Handler skill:** moderate. Luring dependence is the common trap.
- **Best fit:** everything.
- **Failure modes:** bribery; late lure fading; low reinforcement rate in hard environments; no interrupter plan.

#### B. LIMA and the Humane Hierarchy

- A decision procedure, not a technique set. LIMA adopted in IAABC and CCPDT ethics. Humane Hierarchy (Friedman): (1) health, nutrition, environment; (2) antecedent arrangement; (3) R+; (4) DRA; (5) P-, R-, extinction; (6) P+.
- Ziv (2017, J Vet Behav 19:50-60) recommended LIMA after reviewing 17 studies.
- Disagreement: critics argue LIMA permits aversives "if needed"; some force-free bodies reject levels 5 to 6.

#### C. "Balanced" training

- **Mechanism:** R+ to teach; P+ (leash correction, prong, e-collar) and R- (stim released on compliance) to proof and suppress.
- **Evidence:** Cooper et al. (2014, PLOS ONE 9:e102722; DEFRA AW1402a): 92% of owners reported improvement, no group difference; e-collar dogs more stress behavior, especially at high settings. China, Mills and Cooper (2020, Front Vet Sci 7:508): recall and chasing problems; reward group shorter latencies, fewer repeated commands; authors concluded e-collars unnecessary. Elliffe et al. (2021) criticized statistics and Day 5 handler switch. Vieira de Castro 2020: Mixed group poorer welfare during training than Reward group.
- **Effectiveness:** not superior in any controlled pet-dog comparison. Some field reports (NZ kiwi aversion) claim value for avoidance tasks; not controlled.
- **Welfare risks:** pain, fear, conditioned fear of handler or context, redirected aggression, suppression without addressing emotion. Scale with intensity and frequency.
- **Handler skill:** very high.
- **Failure modes:** escalating intensity; aversives on fear-based behavior; poisoned cues.

#### D. Compulsion / traditional and dominance theory

- **Mechanism:** P+ and R- (choke-chain jerks, physical placement). Justified by dominance model.
- **Scientific status:** Mech (1999, Can J Zool 77:1196-1203): alpha model from unrelated captive wolves; wild packs are families; no dominance contests over 13 summers. Bradshaw (2011), Yin: most "dominance" behavior better explained by learning, fear, resource value. Dominance as a relationship descriptor over specific resources remains legitimate; the human-dog status-struggle model and derived methods are rejected. AVSAB advises against.
- **Evidence of harm:** Herron, Shofer and Reisner (2009, Appl Anim Behav Sci 117:47-54): aggressive response by technique: hit or kick 43%; growl at dog 41%; force item from mouth 39%; alpha roll 31%; stare down 30%; dominance down 29%; grab jowls and shake 26%; non-confrontational methods 0 to 6%.
- **Best fit:** none for pet dogs.
- **Failure modes:** aggression toward handler; learned helplessness.

#### E. Relationship-based, social learning, Control Unleashed, BAT

- **Do As I Do (Fugazza):** Fugazza and Miklosi 2014 found comparable or faster than shaping for some object tasks. Moderate for specific tasks, thin generally.
- **Control Unleashed (McDevitt):** Look at That, mat work, pattern games. Mechanism: classical CC plus operant alternatives plus predictability. Evidence: practitioner consensus; no controlled trials found.
- **BAT 2.0 (Stewart):** functional reinforcement and choice; dog explores below threshold on long line; handler follows; "mark and move." Evidence thin; case-based.
- **Failure modes:** working too close; inconsistent setups; owners unable to read threshold.

#### F. Applied behavior analysis framing

- Functional assessment (ABC), single-subject designs, differential reinforcement, schedules, extinction with replacement. Extensive human and lab evidence; dog single-case studies exist. Strength: measurement. Weakness: can underweight emotion, pain, ethology.

## Part 2: Behavior Modification

### 2.1 Universal assessment steps

1. **Veterinary rule-out first.** Mills et al. (2020, Animals 10:318) reviewed 100 recent dog cases from each of several authors' referral caseloads; conservative estimate "around a third of referred cases involve some form of painful condition," individual caseloads 23% to 82%. Treat suspected pain first. Musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, dermatological pain most common. Other rule-outs: thyroid, neurological, urinary (house soiling), dental (mouthing, chewing), sensory decline. A 6 to 8 week analgesic trial is a practical diagnostic.
2. **Functional assessment (ABC):** antecedents, operationally defined behavior, consequences; hypothesize function.
3. **Safety and management** before training.
4. **Baseline measurement** before intervention.

### 2.2 CC/DS mechanics

Desensitization: trigger below threshold, increase gradually. Counterconditioning: pair trigger with something valued so the CER changes; trigger first, then food; food contingent on the trigger, not the behavior. Signs of progress: conditioned look-back, looser body, faster recovery. Standard of care; controlled support mainly in noise-fear contexts; most specific protocols consensus.

### 2.3 Named protocols

Look at That (McDevitt), engage-disengage, pattern games, Relaxation Protocol (Overall 1997, 15-day program), Treat and Retreat (Clothier with Yin), BAT 2.0 (Stewart), Mine! (Donaldson 2002). All consensus; no controlled trials located.

### 2.4 Problem-by-problem guidance

**Leash reactivity:** assess function (fear vs frustrated greeting), threshold distance, pain and vision; management, CC/DS, LAT or engage-disengage, pattern games, BAT, emergency U-turn; avoid leash corrections (Herron 2009); weeks for management, 3 to 12 months for durable change; measure threshold distance, recovery time, reactions per walk; refer on bites or no progress after 8 to 12 weeks.

**Fear and anxiety:** safe haven, sound masking, CC/DS with graded recordings (less effective for storms), medication when severe. Dexmedetomidine gel (Sileo, FDA approved 2015-11-19): Korpivaara et al. 2017 (Vet Rec), RCT, 182 dogs, OR 3.4 for good effect. Imepitoin: Engel et al. 2019 (JVIM), RCT n = 238, OR 4.69 for good or excellent effect; EU approved. Gabapentin for storms: Bleuer-Elsner et al. 2021 (Vet Rec), crossover RCT, 18 dogs, significant reduction, 3 of 18 worse; thin. Generalized anxiety: daily SSRI or TCA with behavior modification; 4 to 6 weeks to effect.

**Separation anxiety:** video the dog alone; rule out boredom, house-training gaps, noise fear, confinement distress. DeMartini approach (consensus): suspend over-threshold absences, graduated departures below threshold with live video, progress by seconds to minutes, several short sessions weekly with rest days, no cry-it-out. Crating: no good evidence it helps; some dogs injure themselves; thin. Medication: King et al. 2000, RCT, 95 dogs, clomipramine plus behavior therapy improved signs; Podberscek et al. 1999 found no advantage over placebo with behavior therapy. Simpson et al. 2007, 242 randomized, 197 evaluable, 72% improvement with fluoxetine vs 50% placebo, both with behavior management. Large placebo-plus-behavior effect. Timeline months, often 4 to 12. Measure minutes alone before first stress sign.

**Resource guarding:** assess items, people, warning sequence; dental and GI pain. Management, Mine!, trade games, R+ drop. Never forcibly remove items (39% aggressive response, Herron 2009). Weeks to months; refer on bites or children.

**Aggression:** bite history by Dunbar scale (1 snap no contact; 2 tooth contact no puncture; 3 one to four shallow punctures; 4 deep punctures or shaking; 5 multiple level 4; 6 fatal; prognosis worsens from level 3), triggers, predictability, victims, household risk, medical workup, ladder of aggression. Safety: R+ muzzle training, strict management; Shikashio on realistic goals. Protocol: CC/DS, DRA, cooperative care, medication. Avoid punishing warnings. Refer for any level 3+, children, unpredictable aggression.

**Impulse control and overarousal:** capture calm, mat work and Relaxation Protocol, It's Yer Choice (Garrett), structured play with rules, adequate sleep (adults often 12+ hours). Consensus; thin research.

**Jumping:** DRI (sit or four-on-floor) plus P- plus management; household consistency. Consensus.

**Pulling:** reinforce position, stop or redirect on tight leash, penalty yards, Premack sniff breaks; front-clip harness; choke and prong concern given case reports (Grohmann et al. 2013, brain damage after choke-chain hanging). Consensus with case-level harm evidence.

**Barking:** assess function (alert, demand, fear, boredom, separation); antecedent control, CC/DS for alert, extinction plus DRA for demand, enrichment; avoid spray and shock bark collars (ESVCE 2018: no credible evidence). Consensus.

**House training:** scheduled outings, immediate outdoor reinforcement, supervision or confinement, enzymatic cleaner, no punishment; crate as pleasant den (AVSAB 2008 endorses confinement training); most puppies reliable by 4 to 6 months; regression: check UTI.

**Destructive chewing:** rule out SRB, pain, under-stimulation; legal chews, rotation, supervision, trade. Consensus.

**Puppy mouthing:** redirect, brief P- on hard bites, manage overtired puppies, bite inhibition before 4 to 5 months (Dunbar). Consensus; normal and declines with age.

**Recall:** new cue with jackpots, long line, Premack, games, gradual distance and distraction, never call for unpleasant things. China 2020 is the strongest direct evidence.

**Cooperative care:** start-button behaviors, CC/DS to handling, happy visits, Fear Free. Pre-visit medication: trazodone, Kim et al. 2022 (JAVMA 260:876-883), crossover RCT 20 dogs, lowered some stress scores not cortisol; gabapentin, Stollar et al. 2022 (JAVMA 260:1031-1040), 22 dogs, only lip licking dropped; combination gabapentin, melatonin, acepromazine, Costa et al. 2023 (JAVMA 261:1660-1665), 45 dogs, no placebo arm; dexmedetomidine gel at vet visits, Korpivaara et al. 2021, randomized, 40.7% excellent vs 4.3% placebo; Clark and Cameron 2025 concluded multimodal approaches better supported. Verdict: thin to moderate.

### 2.5 Medication summary

| Drug | Main use | Best evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fluoxetine | Separation anxiety, generalized anxiety | Simpson 2007 RCT, 242 dogs | Moderate to strong for SA |
| Clomipramine | Separation anxiety, compulsive disorders | King 2000 RCT, 95; Podberscek 1999 null | Moderate (mixed) |
| Dexmedetomidine gel | Noise aversion, vet visits | Korpivaara 2017 RCT, 182; 2021 vet-visit RCT | Moderate to strong |
| Imepitoin | Noise fear | Engel 2019 RCT, 238 | Moderate |
| Trazodone | Situational anxiety | Kim 2022 crossover (20); Gilbert-Gregory 2016 (120, non-randomized); Gruen and Sherman 2008 (56, retrospective) | Thin to moderate |
| Gabapentin | Situational fear, storm phobia | Stollar 2022 (22); Bleuer-Elsner 2021 (18) | Thin |

Medication is an adjunct that lowers fear enough for learning; not a substitute for behavior modification; requires a veterinarian.

## Part 3: Development and Context

**Puppy socialization (about 3 to 14 weeks).** AVSAB 2008: classes at 7 to 8 weeks; minimum one set of vaccines at least 7 days before first class plus first deworming; standard of care to socialize before vaccination complete because behavior problems are a greater threat than infectious disease. Quality over quantity; avoid high-traffic dog areas with unknown vaccination status.

**Adolescence (about 6 to 18 months).** Asher et al. (2020, Biol Lett 16:20200097), 69 dogs tested at 5 and 8 months: at 8 months longer latency to "sit" from caregiver but not stranger; caregivers rated trainability lower than at 5 or 12 months; stronger in insecurely attached dogs; temporary. Limitation: guide-dog candidates of a few breeds. Implication: lower criteria, raise reinforcement, keep consistency.

**Adult rescue dogs and decompression.** The 3-3-3 rule is a shelter heuristic; no peer-reviewed basis found. A low-demand predictable initial period is sensible; individual timelines vary.

**Senior dogs.** Canine cognitive dysfunction: DISHA signs. Arthritis pain often masquerades as grumpiness. Enrichment, diet, medication (selegiline licensed in some countries) have some support; moderate to thin. Old dogs learn with R+.

**Breed and genetics.** Morrill et al. (2022, Science 376:eabk0639), 18,385 dog surveys, 2,155 genomes: most behavioral traits heritable (h2 > 25%); "breed explains just 9% of behavioral variation in individuals"; 11 loci associated; breed almost uninformative for agonistic threshold; biddability tracks breed ancestry more. Implication: breed shifts probabilities; train the individual.

**Enrichment and exercise.** Sniffing, foraging, chewing, appropriate exercise reduce problem behaviors; many specific claims thin; overexercise can backfire; sleep often neglected.

**Nutrition and treats.** About 10% of daily calories as treats; use part of meal ration; small soft high-value pieces. Diet claims for behavior have limited evidence.

**Handler state and consistency.** Inconsistent owners had less obedient dogs (Arhant et al. 2010; Rooney and Cowan 2011). Owner depression associated with more aversive use (Dodman et al. 2018, PLOS ONE, 1,564 owners). Write one household cue dictionary.

## Part 4: Measurement and Comparison

### 4.1 What to measure

| Metric | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Latency | Seconds from cue to onset | Fluency; China 2020 main outcome |
| First-cue compliance | % performed on first cue | Reliability |
| Rate of responding | Correct responses per minute | Fluency, shaping |
| % correct per session | Successes / trials | Criteria decisions |
| Duration | Seconds held | Stays, mat, separation |
| Threshold distance | Closest distance with relaxed behavior | Reactivity, fear |
| Recovery time | Seconds to baseline after trigger | Resilience |
| Stress scores | Fear Free FAS (0 to 5); stress behavior counts | Welfare during training |

Body-language tools: Fear Free FAS scale (practical; peer-reviewed validation limited); ladder of aggression (Shepherd, illustrated by Chin; educational heuristic); Rugaas calming signals (empirical support thin and contested; treat as stress indicators). Questionnaires: C-BARQ (Hsu and Serpell 2003), best validated; use at baseline and follow-up.

### 4.2 Designing a fair comparison

1. Operationally define behavior and primary outcome. 2. Baseline at least 3 sessions. 3. Designs: reversal (A-B-A-B) for management; multiple baseline across behaviors or settings; alternating treatments with distinct cues; group comparisons with randomization, matching, blinded video scoring (all China 2020 dogs wore e-collars so analysts were blind). 4. Control trainer skill, total time, reward value, spacing. 5. Measure efficacy and welfare (latency, % correct, stress behaviors; cortisol and cognitive bias where feasible). 6. Follow up at 4 weeks or more. 7. Report effect sizes and intervals.

## Recommendations

1. Adopt R+ under LIMA / Humane Hierarchy as default; present balanced and compulsion as compared, not recommended.
2. "Vet and pain check first" atop every behavior page (Mills 2020).
3. Tag every claim: Strong / Moderate / Consensus / Thin.
4. Recommend short, spaced sessions (Demant 2011) with the lab-dog caveat.
5. Correct legal misinformation: England has not banned e-collars as of October 2026; date-stamp the legal table.
6. Build measurement templates into protocol pages.

## Caveats

- Most comparative studies are observational or quasi-experimental; owners self-select methods; reverse causation possible.
- Controlled trials are few and small; the two key e-collar studies come from one program; one has a published critique.
- Many popular protocols are expert consensus without controlled trials: a gap, not evidence against.
- Medication trials are often industry-funded; placebo-plus-behavior groups improve substantially.
- Legal status is changing; several entries from secondary sources need statute-level verification.
- Certification hour requirements, Fear Free validation status, and a few citations (Podberscek 1999, Grohmann 2013) were not re-verified and should be checked before publication.
